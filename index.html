Understanding Vermont's Zoning Landscape

Vermont's housing regulations vary significantly. Many towns restrict housing to single-family homes on large lots, which affects the availability of homes in walkable places—apartments near downtown, duplexes in village centers, or accessory dwelling units.

Vermont is unique. Unlike most states where counties control zoning, Vermont grants each of its 255 towns the authority to create its own bylaws. Each municipality crafts rules about what can be built, where, and how—dividing their land into zoning districts with specific regulations.

Fair Housing Requirement: Vermont law (24 VSA § 4412) requires municipalities to ensure zoning regulations provide reasonable opportunity for all housing types and don't have discriminatory effects.

Vermont's Housing Data

The state's 2025 Housing Needs Assessment documents Vermont's housing market conditions. Population has grown slowly over decades, while housing production has grown at a different rate, affecting housing costs and availability.

Key Findings from the 2025 Housing Needs Assessment

  • Vermont needs an estimated 24,000-36,000 additional year-round homes by 2029 to meet demand, normalize vacancy rates, house the homeless, and replace homes lost from the stock
  • Vacancy rates declined to near record levels following the COVID-19 pandemic
  • Home prices and rents increased significantly between 2020-2025
  • Homelessness increased dramatically during this period
  • Vermont maintains a long-standing shortage of affordable homes available to lower-income households

Source: Vermont Department of Housing and Community Development, 2025 Statewide Housing Needs Assessment

Zoning regulations affect housing production. Regulations that require large lot sizes, prohibit apartments and multi-family homes, or restrict accessory dwelling units affect what housing can be built. When only certain housing types are allowed, this affects the range of housing options available.

Act 250: Vermont's State-Level Development Review

While local zoning controls what can be built in each district, Vermont also has a unique state-level land use review process called Act 250. Enacted in 1970 during a period of rapid development pressure, Act 250 reviews the environmental, social, and fiscal impacts of major developments.

How Act 250 Works

Act 250 permits are required for larger projects—typically developments over 10 acres, commercial projects on more than 1 acre (or 10 acres in municipalities without zoning), and housing projects with 10 or more units. Nine District Environmental Commissions, staffed by citizen volunteers appointed by the Governor, review applications against 10 statutory criteria covering:

  • Water and air quality impacts
  • Effect on water supplies and soil erosion
  • Traffic and transportation
  • Educational services burden
  • Municipal and governmental services
  • Scenic and natural beauty
  • Historic sites and conformance with local plans
  • Primary agricultural soils and forests
  • Energy efficiency and renewable energy impacts
  • Wildlife habitat and natural areas (new criterion effective 12/31/2026)

Act 250 & Local Zoning: Two Layers of Review

A project may comply with local zoning yet still need an Act 250 permit if it exceeds size thresholds. Conversely, receiving an Act 250 permit doesn't replace the need for local zoning approval. The two systems work in parallel—local zoning ensures projects fit community plans, while Act 250 addresses broader environmental and regional impacts. The Vermont Zoning Atlas focuses on local zoning regulations, which apply to all projects regardless of size.

Act 181: Vermont's 2024 Housing and Land Use Reforms

In 2024, Vermont enacted Act 181, a comprehensive housing and land use reform law. The act updates Act 250 governance, shifts Act 250 jurisdiction to designated locations, and sets minimum zoning standards for municipalities. The summary below focuses on provisions most relevant to local zoning and state review.

Key Provisions Affecting Local Zoning

Act 250 Governance Update

Creates a five-member Land Use Review Board and reorganizes Act 250 administration under a professional board structure.

Act 250 Location-Based Jurisdiction

Shifts Act 250 review to a location-based system that exempts development in designated downtowns, village centers, and neighborhood development areas while strengthening protections for forests, agricultural lands, and wildlife habitat outside these areas.

Parking Requirement Reforms

Requires municipalities to eliminate or significantly reduce parking minimums for housing in designated areas (downtowns, village centers, neighborhood development areas). Municipalities may set maximum parking limits instead.

Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU) Standards

Requires municipalities to allow one ADU by right on lots with an existing single-family home, subject to applicable health and safety, wastewater, historic district, and dimensional standards. Limits certain local restrictions, including some owner-occupancy and parking requirements.

Missing Middle Housing in Villages

Requires municipalities with designated village centers to allow duplexes and small multi-family housing within those areas.

Neighborhood Development Areas

Creates a state designation for multi-acre sites intended for neighborhood-scale housing. Projects in these areas receive streamlined permitting and Act 250 exemptions.

Local Option Tax for Housing

Authorizes municipalities to adopt up to 3% local option tax on meals, alcohol, and lodging, with revenues dedicated to affordable housing, childcare, and municipal services.

Implementation Timeline

Act 181 provisions are being implemented in phases from 2024-2027. Many requirements took effect in 2025, with municipalities required to update their zoning bylaws to comply. The Vermont Zoning Atlas will be updated annually to reflect how communities implement these reforms.

Why Act 181 Matters for the Atlas: This legislation will fundamentally reshape Vermont's zoning landscape over the next few years. The atlas provides a baseline to measure how Act 181's requirements change local regulations, track which communities embrace reform, and identify where barriers to housing persist despite new state mandates.

Understanding Housing Types: What the Atlas Tracks

The Vermont Zoning Atlas maps what types of housing each district allows. Understanding these housing categories is essential for interpreting the data and identifying where incremental zoning reforms could expand opportunities.

Data Fields in the Atlas

For each of Vermont's 1,755 zoning districts, the atlas documents regulations for these key housing types:

Two- and three-family homes face significant barriers. These traditional Vermont housing types—common in village centers and mill towns—are often prohibited or require conditional use permits in many districts. This "missing middle" housing provides naturally affordable options between single-family homes and larger apartments.

Key Finding: Duplexes require public hearings or are prohibited in many residential districts. This affects where 2-3 family homes can be built, including in village centers and walkable neighborhoods.

Multi-Family Housing: Four Units and Above

Apartments are heavily restricted. Four-or-more-unit buildings (apartments) face the most significant zoning barriers. Many towns prohibit multi-family housing entirely or allow it only in limited commercial/mixed-use zones, often far from services and transit.

Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs)

ADU regulations now follow statewide requirements. Vermont law requires by-right ADUs on lots with an existing single-family home, subject to applicable health and safety, wastewater, historic district, and dimensional standards. Municipal rules still shape size, placement, and parking where allowed by statute.

ADU Context: Changes to ADU regulations affect how many rental units can be built. ADUs can provide income for homeowners and create rental units at various price points.

Lot Size Requirements and Land Costs

Large minimum lot sizes increase the land cost component of housing. Many Vermont towns require 1-3 acre minimums even in areas with municipal water and sewer service.

Context: Requiring a $100,000 lot instead of a $50,000 lot directly increases the minimum price of a home. Lot size requirements affect the economics of housing development.

Regional Focus: Chittenden County

Vermont's most populous region contains 19 municipalities with 168,000 residents. The Burlington metropolitan area provides a case study of how zoning regulations vary across an economically integrated region.

Regional Context

This region contains Vermont's largest job market, the University of Vermont, the state's main hospital complex, and tech sector employers. Housing market indicators include:

  • Rental vacancy rates below 1% (compared to 3-5% in balanced markets)
  • Median home prices exceeding $450,000 in many towns
  • Significant commuting distances for many workers
  • Documentation of outmigration in some age groups

What Zoning Shows

Single-family homes dominate allowances: Like the rest of Vermont, single-family homes are permitted widely, but many town require large lots even in villages close to jobs and transit.

Multi-family housing is limited: Several bedroom communities surrounding Burlington prohibit apartments entirely or restrict them to small overlay zones. This pushes all multi-family development into Burlington and a few surrounding towns, concentrating housing in limited areas.

Example: South Burlington

Vermont's second-largest city has significant commercial development. Residential zoning includes 1-acre minimum lots in many areas. Multi-family housing is restricted to specific zones. The city contains major employers with thousands of jobs.

Example: Williston

A major commercial corridor along Route 2 includes big-box retail and office parks. Residential zoning emphasizes large-lot single-family homes. The town has significant employment but limited housing near job centers.

Regulatory Variations in Chittenden County

The atlas reveals several common patterns in Chittenden County zoning:

  • Duplexes often require public hearings or are prohibited in residential zones
  • Lot size minimums typically range from ½ acre to 2+ acres, even where municipal water/sewer exists
  • Historic village centers have varying approaches to mixed-use and multi-family housing
  • ADU rules follow statewide by-right requirements, with remaining variation in dimensional limits, parking, and wastewater constraints
  • Small multi-family (4-8 units) allowances differ significantly near transit and commercial areas

Zoning for Great Neighborhoods

This atlas supports Vermont's "Zoning for Great Neighborhoods" initiative, led by the Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD) in partnership with the Congress for the New Urbanism. The initiative provides resources for municipalities considering zoning changes.

Zoning rules affect planning outcomes related to community integration, climate resilience, transportation access, and housing development. By mapping what's currently allowed across all 255 towns, the Vermont Zoning Atlas enables:

  • Researchers and advocates to identify patterns in housing regulations
  • Town officials and planning commissions to compare their bylaws to other communities
  • Policy makers to understand statewide patterns in zoning regulations
  • Citizens to see what's allowed in their community

Next Steps: Using This Atlas

Our Position on Local Control: The Vermont Zoning Atlas does not take a position in the debate between those who advocate for local control and those who argue for stronger state limits on local governance. We provide data to inform policy discussions at the local and state levels. How communities approach zoning is for local decision-makers and citizens to determine.

Instead, we provide transparent data to inform conversations about housing in Vermont. This atlas is a tool for understanding current regulations—what they allow, where barriers exist, and how communities compare to one another.

How Communities Can Use This Data

For Municipal Officials

The atlas enables comparison of your town's zoning to similar communities and neighboring towns. Users can review regulations across Vermont to understand the range of approaches to housing regulation.

For Planning Commissions

Statewide data provides context for bylaw review and updates. When considering changes to multi-family allowances or lot size requirements, the atlas shows how proposals compare to regulations elsewhere in Vermont. RPCs can assist with local data analysis.

For Researchers & Advocates

All data is open-source and available for download. Conduct statistical analyses, create visualizations, or integrate with other datasets. Help us improve the atlas by submitting corrections and updates.

For Citizens

The atlas shows what types of housing are allowed in your town. Data from the atlas can inform participation in planning commission meetings and local policy discussions.

Common Zoning Reform Topics in Vermont

Based on atlas findings and documented zoning changes in Vermont communities, discussions at the local level have included:

  • Duplex allowances in single-family zones
  • Minimum lot sizes where municipal water/sewer exists
  • ADU approval processes and restrictions
  • Small multi-family (4-8 units) allowances in village centers and near transit
  • Parking minimum requirements near downtowns
  • "Missing middle" housing districts for traditional neighborhood development

Note: Vermont towns have different priorities and approaches to zoning. The atlas documents current regulations to inform local decision-making processes.

Keeping the Atlas Current

Zoning regulations change regularly. Our goal is to update the atlas annually, but we need community help. If your town has updated its bylaws, please let us know. Municipal clerks, planning staff, and RPC representatives can submit updates through our GitHub repository or contact form.

The Vermont Zoning Atlas is part of the National Zoning Atlas Project, led by Professor Sara Bronin.

About This Project

The Vermont Zoning Atlas brings together zoning laws from 1,755 districts across all municipalities. Without this tool, one would have to read thousands of pages of dense legal code to answer simple questions about where and what types of housing can be built.
